Section 01

Overview

CAES defines the minimum structural requirements for AI systems executing actions that carry legal, financial, regulatory, or operational consequence. A CAES-conformant system does not merely log what happened — it proves, before execution occurs, that the action was authorized.

Artificial intelligence systems have crossed from advisory to operational capability. They now commit transactions, modify infrastructure, and make decisions at a scale and velocity that exceeds human review. This shift introduces a structural accountability gap: the absence of a formal standard for what it means to authorize an AI-initiated action and prove that authorization occurred.

CAES addresses this gap. It does not prescribe implementation architecture. It defines the normative properties that any conformant system must provide — regardless of the underlying technology, deployment model, or AI provider.

This specification is normative. The keyword MUST indicates an absolute requirement. MUST NOT indicates an absolute prohibition. SHOULD indicates a recommendation. MAY indicates an option. Usage follows RFC 2119.
Section 02

Scope

This specification applies to any AI system — agent, model, or automated pipeline — that:

(a) Produces effects beyond the boundary of its own process state (see Section 4: Effect Boundary);
(b) Operates in an environment subject to regulatory, legal, or financial accountability;
(c) Executes actions that a human or policy authority must be able to verify after the fact.

CAES does not apply to purely advisory systems — those whose output requires explicit human mediation before real-world effect occurs. The boundary is the Effect Boundary: if the AI can cause effects without a human completing a separate, independent action, CAES scope applies.

An Effect Boundary is crossed even when the AI system does not directly execute the operation, but causes it through an intermediary — including human operators, automation platforms, schedulers, or downstream services. See Section 4 for the complete definition.
Section 03

Definitions

Defined Terms
Decision Receipt
A cryptographically signed, serialized artifact produced at policy evaluation time. Constitutes proof that authorization was evaluated before execution. Not a log entry.
PolicyHash
A deterministic hash of the canonical policy state active at evaluation time. Computed from a canonical representation such that semantically identical policies yield identical hashes. Enables independent verification that policy has not been altered since the decision was made.
Effect Boundary
A boundary crossed when an AI system's output produces, initiates, or materially influences effects outside the agent's isolated computation context. See Section 4 for the complete normative definition.
Execution Gate
The enforcement point at which a verified Decision Receipt is required before any action is permitted to proceed. Absence of receipt must result in execution denial.
Governed Spine
An append-only, causally ordered, tenant-scoped record of all governed events. Serves as the authoritative forensic record. Not a log. Mutations are not permitted. Every event carries causal parent reference(s) sufficient to reconstruct a deterministic partial order.
Trigger
The originating event that created the execution scope. In cases of retry, the retry references the original Trigger identity. A new Trigger creates a new spine. Scheduled or multi-source fan-in events each constitute a distinct Trigger.
Denial Receipt
An affirmative governance artifact produced when an action is denied. Constitutes proof that the constraint functioned as designed. Structurally equivalent to an approval receipt.
Fail-Closed
System behavior under ambiguity or failure: when authorization cannot be verified, the action does not proceed. Uncertainty resolves to denial, never to silent permission.
Human Authority
A cryptographically bound delegation by a human operator authorizing a specific action. Not an approval click. A signed, narrow, scoped authorization artifact with explicit temporal validity.
Section 04

Effect Boundary

An Effect Boundary is crossed when an AI system's output produces, initiates, or materially influences effects outside the agent's isolated computation context. This definition is the "no loopholes" clause. It prevents claiming advisory status while producing consequential effects.

An Effect Boundary event occurs when any of the following categories of effect are produced, initiated, or materially influenced by an AI system — whether directly or through an intermediary (human operator, automation platform, scheduler, or downstream service).

Effect Categories

Normative — Effect Boundary Categories
1
Digital State Change

Mutations to systems of record, configuration, data stores, access controls, financial systems, or infrastructure.

2
External System Interaction

Any API call, network request, message publish, job enqueue, tool invocation, or command execution against a system not wholly contained within the agent process.

3
Information Release / Communication Effect

Any output delivered to a human or external party that can reasonably be acted upon as instruction, authorization, representation, or decision — including email, chat, voice, UI output, or generated documents.

4
Human Contact / Behavioral Influence

Any interaction intended or likely to influence human behavior, perception, consent, or decision-making in a consequential context — e.g., medical guidance, financial direction, legal instruction, HR decisions, safety-critical commands.

5
Physical Actuation

Any action that controls or influences physical systems: motors, vehicles, robots, machinery, smart-home devices, industrial equipment, access gates, drones, or other actuators — whether directly or through intermediary controllers.

6
Cross-Boundary Read (Conditional)

Pure read operations are generally out of scope unless they cross a tenant boundary, a privilege boundary, or expose regulated/controlled data, in which case the read itself constitutes an Effect Boundary event.

An Effect Boundary is crossed even when the AI system does not directly execute the operation, but causes it through an intermediary. This is the structural guarantee that prevents advisory-framing loopholes.

Effect Boundary Sub-Classifications

To support proportionate governance requirements, CAES defines two sub-classifications of Effect Boundary:

Recommended Sub-Terms
Effect Boundary — Hard: Digital state mutation + physical actuation + external system interaction. Categories 1, 2, and 5. Direct, measurable, typically irreversible effects on systems or the physical world.
Effect Boundary — Soft: Communication effect + behavioral influence. Categories 3 and 4. Effects mediated through human interpretation, but consequential in regulated or safety-critical contexts.

Compliance tiers may reference these sub-classifications to establish proportionate requirements. For example, an implementation governing a customer support chatbot (Soft effects) may have different evidence requirements than one governing autonomous trading execution (Hard effects). Both remain within CAES scope.

CAES explicitly treats speech, human influence, robotics, and vehicles as first-class effect domains. Category 6 (Cross-Boundary Read) applies conditionally based on boundary and data classification.
Section 05

Normative Requirements

The following requirements are normative. A system claiming CAES conformance must satisfy all requirements at the applicable compliance level.

5.1 — Authorization Requirements

CAES-AUTH
AUTH-01
Every action crossing an Effect Boundary (as defined in Section 4) MUST be preceded by a policy evaluation.
AUTH-02
Policy evaluation MUST produce a Decision Receipt before execution proceeds.
AUTH-03
The Decision Receipt MUST contain a deterministic PolicyHash bound to the policy state at evaluation time.
AUTH-03a
PolicyHash MUST be computed from a canonical policy representation such that semantically identical policies yield identical hashes. Field ordering, whitespace, and encoding must not affect the resulting hash value.
AUTH-04
Execution MUST fail closed if a verified Decision Receipt cannot be produced.
AUTH-05
There MUST be no implicit authorization path. Absence of receipt equals absence of authority.

5.2 — Evidence Requirements

CAES-EVID
EVID-01
Decision Receipts MUST be cryptographically signed and independently verifiable.
EVID-01a
Decision Receipts MUST be verifiable using a public key or equivalent trust bundle independent of the runtime that produced the receipt. Offline verification MUST be possible without access to the production system.
EVID-02
Denial decisions MUST produce a Denial Receipt equivalent in structural integrity to an approval receipt.
EVID-03
The governed spine MUST be append-only. Canonical records MUST NOT be mutated, overwritten, or soft-deleted.
EVID-03a
Every spine event MUST carry causal parent reference(s) sufficient to reconstruct a deterministic partial order. Tenant scope MUST be declared on every spine event.
EVID-04
A terminal outcome record MUST exist for every executed action. Progress telemetry does not satisfy this requirement.
EVID-05
Evidence artifacts MUST be verifiable without access to the live production system.

5.3 — Failure Requirements

CAES-FAIL
FAIL-01
System failure during authorization MUST resolve to execution denial, never silent continuation.
FAIL-02
Failure events MUST be recorded. Silent degradation is a conformance violation.
FAIL-03
Partial execution state MUST NOT persist when spine append fails prior to action execution.
Section 06

Core Primitives

CAES defines three structural primitives. These are the minimum objects a conformant system must be able to produce, persist, and present to an auditor.

Primitive 1 — The Decision Receipt

The Decision Receipt is the atomic unit of governed authorization. It is produced at evaluation time — not after execution — and binds the following: the identity of the requesting actor, the canonical policy state (via PolicyHash), the disposition (Approve / Deny / RequiresHuman), the timestamp of evaluation, and a cryptographic signature verifiable via a public key or trust bundle independent of the runtime.

A Decision Receipt is not a log entry. It is an authorization artifact. The distinction is structural: logs record what happened. A Decision Receipt proves that authorization was evaluated before it happened.

Primitive 2 — The PolicyHash

The PolicyHash is a deterministic, reproducible hash of the canonical policy state active at the moment of evaluation. It must be computed from a canonical representation such that semantically identical policies — regardless of field ordering, whitespace, or encoding — yield identical hashes. Any party with access to the policy definition can independently recompute the hash. A PolicyHash mismatch is proof of policy drift or manipulation.

Primitive 3 — The Governed Spine

The Governed Spine is the append-only, causally ordered, tenant-scoped record of all governed events. It is not a log. It is the forensic source of truth. Every event in the spine carries: a unique immutable identifier, a causal parent reference sufficient to reconstruct a deterministic partial order, a tenant scope declaration, a hash commitment over the payload, and a timestamp assigned at ingestion.

Section 07

Causal Chain

Every governed operation produces a spine — a causally ordered sequence of governed events. The spine is the complete forensic record of a single operational run.

The canonical CAES causal chain is:

Trigger → Intent → Justification → Decision → Action → Outcome

Chain Invariants
CI-01
Exactly one Trigger per spine. A Trigger is the originating event that created the execution scope. Retries MUST reference the original Trigger identity rather than creating a new spine. Scheduled invocations and multi-source fan-in events each constitute a distinct Trigger.
CI-02
Exactly one terminal Outcome per Action. Progress telemetry MUST NOT satisfy the terminal outcome requirement.
CI-03
No Action without a preceding Decision with a valid Decision Receipt.
CI-04
No spine object is mutable after promotion to the authoritative ledger.
CI-05
Every spine event MUST carry causal parent reference(s) sufficient to reconstruct the complete deterministic partial order of the operation.
Section 08

Authority Model

CAES does not prescribe how policy is authored. It defines how authority is exercised and proven.

When high-stakes actions require human review, a CAES-conformant system must not merely pause and resume. It must produce a cryptographic signing request. The human operator must produce a signed delegation — narrow in scope, bound to the specific action, and recorded in the governed spine.

Human authority in a CAES system is:

Scoped — authorization applies to a specific action, not a class of actions. Temporal — the delegation has explicit validity bounds. Non-repudiable — the operator's identity is cryptographically bound. Recorded — the delegation is a spine event, not an out-of-band approval.

The CAES authority model treats humans as governed actors — not system overrides. Dissent, escalation, and override are first-class events in the governed spine, not exceptions to it.
Section 09

Compliance Criteria

CAES conformance is defined in three levels. Higher levels are supersets of lower levels.

Level 1
Receipt-Bounded Execution
Satisfies: AUTH-01AUTH-02AUTH-04AUTH-05EVID-02FAIL-01FAIL-02
Minimum viable CAES conformance. Every execution is receipt-bounded and fail-closed.
Level 2
Verifiable Evidence Chain
Satisfies: Level 1 + AUTH-03AUTH-03aEVID-01EVID-01aEVID-03EVID-03aEVID-04EVID-05FAIL-03
Full cryptographic evidence chain. PolicyHash binding with canonical representation. Offline-verifiable evidence artifacts with independent trust roots.
Level 3
Full Constitutional Conformance
Satisfies: Level 2 + Human Authority Model + Causal Chain Invariants (CI-01 through CI-05) + Full Spine Spec + Effect Boundary classification
Complete CAES implementation. Suitable for regulated industries, legal discovery, audit submission.
CAES does not provide third-party certification at this time. Independent validation against this specification is the responsibility of the implementing organization. The CAES Working Group intends to publish a formal test suite in a future revision.
Section 10

Conformance Claims

Any implementation claiming CAES compliance must publish the following information. This requirement exists to make conformance claims auditable and to prevent dilution of the standard through unverifiable assertions.

Required Conformance Statement
CAES Version
The specification version against which conformance is claimed (e.g., v0.1.1).
Claimed Level
Conformance level: 1 (Receipt-Bounded), 2 (Verifiable Evidence), or 3 (Full Constitutional).
Receipt Verification Method
How Decision Receipts are verified offline. Must identify the trust root or public key mechanism.
PolicyHash Canonicalization
The canonical representation method used for PolicyHash computation (e.g., JCS, sorted-key JSON, custom canonical form).
Fail-Closed Enforcement
How fail-closed behavior is enforced. Must describe the mechanism, not merely assert the property.
Effect Boundary Scope
Which Effect Boundary categories (1–6) the implementation governs, and whether Hard, Soft, or both classifications apply.
Implementations MUST NOT represent themselves as CAES-compliant unless a complete Conformance Statement is published. Conformance statements MUST cite a specific CAES version. Unversioned conformance claims are non-conformant.
Section 11

Non-Compliant Patterns

The following patterns are explicitly non-compliant with CAES at any level. This section exists to prevent dilution of the standard through architectural workarounds that satisfy the letter of individual requirements while violating their intent.

Non-Compliant — Authorization
NCP-01
Prompt-level governance without execution-level enforcement. Instructing an LLM via system prompt to "follow policy" does not constitute policy evaluation. CAES requires a deterministic, mechanically enforced policy evaluation producing a Decision Receipt — not a probabilistic compliance suggestion.
NCP-02
Post-hoc logging as authorization evidence. Recording that an action occurred after execution does not prove authorization was evaluated before execution. CAES requires the Decision Receipt to exist prior to the execution event.
NCP-03
LLM-as-judge without deterministic fallback. Using a language model as the sole policy evaluator — where the evaluation is non-deterministic and non-reproducible — does not satisfy AUTH-03 or AUTH-03a. PolicyHash must be deterministic. A different output on identical inputs is a conformance failure.
NCP-04
Hash-only signing claimed as cryptographic verification. Computing a hash of a receipt without signing it with an asymmetric key does not satisfy EVID-01 or EVID-01a. Hashing proves integrity. Signing proves authority and enables offline verification via an independent trust root.
Non-Compliant — Evidence
NCP-05
Mutable logs presented as governed spine. Any record store that permits overwrite, soft-delete, or last-write-wins semantics is not a governed spine and does not satisfy EVID-03. Append-only is enforced, not declared.
NCP-06
Evidence artifacts that require live system access to verify. If an auditor must connect to a running production instance to validate a receipt or evidence pack, EVID-05 and EVID-01a are not satisfied. Verification must be offline-capable.
NCP-07
Progress telemetry treated as terminal outcome. Status updates, heartbeats, and intermediate state reports do not satisfy CI-02 or EVID-04. Every executed action must produce exactly one terminal outcome record.
Non-Compliant — Scope & Boundary
NCP-08
Advisory framing for systems that produce consequential effects. Claiming a system is "advisory only" while it auto-sends emails, issues voice commands, drives actuators, or publishes decisions that humans act upon without independent verification does not remove the system from CAES scope. See Effect Boundary Section 4, Categories 3, 4, and 5.
NCP-09
Silent degradation under failure. Any system that continues execution when authorization verification fails — whether by configuration, fallback path, or exception handling — violates FAIL-01 and FAIL-02. Failure must resolve to denial with a recorded event.
NCP-10
Governance bypass via invocation surface. Changing how a system is called — in-process, via API, via MCP, via agent-to-agent protocol — does not exempt the execution from governance requirements. CAES applies at the execution layer, not the invocation layer.
This section is intentionally explicit. Its purpose is to prevent the "letter of the law" compliance that undermines standards adoption. Patterns listed here are structural violations — not edge cases. Implementations exhibiting any of these patterns may not claim CAES conformance at any level.

Section 14

Working Group Charter

The CAES Working Group exists to formalize requirements for constitutional AI execution. The Working Group publishes normative specifications defining what a governed AI system must be able to prove — not how it must be built. Specification updates are versioned and publicly documented. Reference implementations may be independently validated against this specification.

Process

Specification amendments are proposed via documented change requests. Each change request must identify: the normative requirement affected, the rationale for change, and backward compatibility implications. Changes are published with the next versioned release. There are no informal amendments.

The Working Group operates on a principle of minimal governance. The specification must remain foundational, normative, and unbloated. CAES defines primitives. Implementations build on them.
Section 15

Amendment Process

CAES does not change silently. Every normative change is versioned. Every version is publicly documented with a summary of what changed and why. The amendment process exists to make the specification more correct — not more comprehensive. Scope creep is not an amendment.

To propose an amendment: document the requirement, the problem it solves, and the conformance implications. Submit to the Working Group. Amendment proposals are acknowledged, considered, and either incorporated into the next versioned draft or formally declined with rationale.
